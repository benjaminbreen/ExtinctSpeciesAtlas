01 / A lost plant

Silphium

Cyrenaica

Ancient writers describe silphium as a valuable plant associated with Cyrenaica. Its botanical identity remains unresolved.

Herodotus · Histories

“From this point begins the silphion and it extends along the coast…”

Place named
From the island of Platea toward the entrance of the Syrtis, along the North African coast.
What it establishes
A reported geographic range. The ancient endpoints are not precise coordinates.

Herodotus describes a coastal span, but the passage does not identify the plant botanically or establish precise modern boundaries for its range.

Pliny · Natural History

02
“Within the memory of the present generation, a single stalk is all that has ever been found there…”
19.15 · Bostock and Riley translation
Explore the map ↓1st century CELatin
Place named
Cyrenaica, with Cyrene and Rome appearing in the account of resin and trade.
What it establishes
Pliny says the Cyrenaic plant had become extremely scarce; he reports a stalk sent to Nero.

This is a report of rarity, not a dated extinction certificate. Pliny also distinguishes the celebrated Cyrenaic product from resins sold from Persia, Media, and Armenia.

Synesius · Letter 106

03
“I asked the young man who brought me some silphium from you, whether you had grown it yourself…”
Letter 106 · A. Fitzgerald translation
Explore the map ↓c. 402 CEGreek
Place named
His brother Euoptius’s garden in the Cyrenaican region, associated with Ptolemais.
What it establishes
The name “silphium” remained in use and a plant so named could be grown there.

Synesius does not say that silphium was nearly gone. His letter complicates a simple last-stalk story: whether his garden plant was the ancient Cyrenaic species remains open.

What the sources do not establish

Pliny reports that the prized Cyrenaic plant had become scarce. Centuries later, Synesius refers to silphium grown in a garden. These accounts may describe different plants under the same name; the texts alone cannot establish their botanical identities.

Later evidence, including coins, herbals, and other written sources, needs to be considered alongside these three starting texts. The dates and circumstances of the plant’s disappearance remain uncertain.

The image

The 1644 woodcuts

The botanical woodcut on this page comes from page 598 of Johannes Bodaeus van Stapel’s 1644 edition of Theophrastus. Its caption calls the plant laserpitium drawn from his garden. On the same printed page, another image compares a proposed plant with a Cyrene coin. These images document early modern attempts to identify silphium; neither verifies the ancient plant’s appearance.
