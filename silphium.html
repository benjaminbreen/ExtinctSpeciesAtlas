What the sources do not establish

Pliny reports that the prized Cyrenaic plant had become scarce. Centuries later, Synesius refers to silphium grown in a garden. These accounts may describe different plants under the same name; the texts alone cannot establish their botanical identities.

The three records here are a starting set. Coins, herbals, medical texts, and further written testimony need to be assessed before making claims about the plant’s appearance or disappearance.

The 1644 woodcuts

The engraving above comes from page 598 of Johannes Bodaeus van Stapel’s 1644 edition of Theophrastus. Its caption calls the plant laserpitium drawn from his garden.
