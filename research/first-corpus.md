# Proposed first corpus

Twelve cases for the atlas's initial research program, including the existing silphium dossier. Each has written testimony from when the organism lived, a disappearance before 1900, and a researchable connection to human activity. These are **dossier candidates**, not claims that the primary-source search is complete. The two Rodrigues tortoises could share one dossier while retaining separate species records.

1. **Silphium of Cyrenaica** — The initial case. [Herodotus 4.169](../source-herodotus.html), [Pliny, *Natural History* 19](../source-pliny.html), and [Synesius, Letter 106](../source-synesius.html) show why the name, biological identity, and late reports have to be investigated together. Its status as one identifiable extinct species remains a research question.
2. **Aurochs (*Bos primigenius*)** — [Caesar, *Gallic War* 6.28](https://www.originalsources.com/Document.aspx?DocID=PD4AJ728MAIT7LU) describes living *uri* and hunting. Later Polish records make the reported 1627 end of the wild population a question about administration and historical species recognition. [Research on the historical record](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/nanna/record/7510/files/Kitchen_uchicago_0330D_17030.pdf?registerDownload=1&version=1&withMetadata=0&withWatermark=0).
3. **Dodo (*Raphus cucullatus*)** — Dutch voyage and settlement accounts document a living bird before its seventeenth-century disappearance. The [Natural History Museum's overview](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/the-dodo-bird-the-real-facts-about-this-icon-of-extinction.html) distinguishes early testimony from later reconstructions and describes hunting and introduced animals.
4. **Rodrigues solitaire (*Pezophaps solitaria*)** — François [Leguat's 1708 voyage](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/36081) is a sustained account of the bird alive. Its unusual later history of being doubted, then recognized from material remains, is documented in a [taxonomic study](https://academic.oup.com/zoolinnean/article/doi/10.1093/zoolinnean/zlae086/7733394).
5. **Steller's sea cow (*Hydrodamalis gigas*)** — Georg Wilhelm Steller observed living animals in 1741 and described their bodies and behavior in [*De bestiis marinis* (1751)](https://siris-bibliographies.si.edu/ipac20/ipac.jsp?booklistformat=&profile=biball&session=177F12701Y613.15521&uri=full%3D3100001~%217965~%210). The [Natural History Museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/stellers-sea-cow-first-historical-extinction-of-marine-mammal-at-human-hands.html) connects their loss by 1768 to hunting along fur-trade routes.
6. **Rodrigues saddle-backed giant tortoise (*Cylindraspis vosmaeri*)** — [Leguat's account](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/bibliography/36081), later illustrations and specimens, and records of tortoise export support a dossier about commercial removal. An [IUCN specialist group's review](https://iucn-tftsg.org/wp-content/uploads/file/Accounts/crm_5_000e_fossil_checklist_v1_2015.pdf) identifies the two Rodrigues species and the scale of eighteenth-century exploitation.
7. **Rodrigues domed giant tortoise (*Cylindraspis peltastes*)** — The same historical archive requires separating this smaller species from the saddle-backed tortoise rather than assigning every reference to “tortoises” to one taxon. The distinctness of both Rodrigues species is supported by [morphological and ancient-DNA research](https://iucn-tftsg.org/wp-content/uploads/file/Articles/Austin_and_Arnold_2001.pdf).
8. **Bluebuck (*Hippotragus leucophaeus*)** — Early Cape reports, Peter Kolb's German and Dutch accounts, and Pallas's 1766 description form a compact multilingual record. [Naturalis' study of Kolb's illustrations](https://repository.naturalis.nl/pub/318339) and [research on surviving specimens and early references](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7822880/) make it a strong case for testing names against objects. The species disappeared around 1800 as colonial land use and hunting intensified.
9. **Mauritius blue pigeon (*Alectroenas nitidissimus*, also printed *nitidissima*)** — Dutch accounts, [Sonnerat's 1782 illustration](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb44555801p), specimens, and later reports allow a dossier on shifting names and last-record dates. The [Natural History Museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/news/2020/november/invasive-species-impact-on-island-flora-and-fauna.html) attributes its nineteenth-century disappearance to deforestation and introduced predators.
10. **Great auk (*Pinguinus impennis*)** — Fishing, hunting, collecting, and natural-history records span centuries and several North Atlantic languages. A [study of an eighteenth-century manuscript](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/oceanography/a8f80ad9-1e77-5ba2-8994-0f3b8753ba66/an-account-of-the-natural-history-and-exploitation-of-the-great-auk-pinguinus-impennis-in-histoire-des-pesches-an-illustrated-eighteenth-century-manuscript) gives a specific textual lead; the [Natural History Museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/worlds-collide-great-auk.html) traces the nineteenth-century collapse and the collecting of rare birds and eggs.
11. **Cuban macaw (*Ara tricolor*)** — Spanish-language natural histories, travel accounts, and specimens make this a Caribbean case with substantial work still to do on the date and place of its last appearances. [Gundlach's Cuban ornithology](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/item/136007) is a primary starting point; [Wiley and Kirwan's source and specimen review](https://boc-online.org/bulletins/downloads/BBOC1332-PDFa.pdf) examines human pressures and the limits of reported dates.
12. **Falkland Islands wolf / warrah (*Dusicyon australis*)** — [Darwin's 1839 account](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1839_Voyage_F10.3/1839_Voyage_F10.3.html) describes the living animal and anticipates its disappearance. The [IUCN assessment](https://doi.org/10.2305/IUCN.UK.2015-4.RLTS.T6923A82310440.en) records persecution and a last reported individual in 1876.

## Deliberate exclusions from this first list

- **Quagga**: an unusually rich historical case, but [the Quagga Project](https://www.quaggaproject.org/) treats it as an extinct *subspecies* of the living plains zebra. Add it if the atlas explicitly expands to infraspecific losses.
- **Moa**: human-driven extinctions, but no known written description made while moa lived, so they do not meet the atlas's documentary gate.
- **Passenger pigeon and Carolina parakeet**: exceptionally well documented, but their final extinctions fall after the stated 1900 cutoff.
- **Labrador duck**: pre-1900 and historically documented, but its extinction cause is less firmly established than the cases above. It remains a useful reserve candidate.

Before turning any candidate into a public dossier, verify its contemporary references at passage level, record the original language and edition, and distinguish a dated specimen, a reported sighting, and a later retrospective statement.
